## Prologue: Two Things That Happened in the Same Morning

This morning I woke up a little hungover, listening to an AI safety researcher on *The Ezra Klein Show* explain why he left Anthropic. His reason wasn't a sci-fi movie villain moment. It was simpler and weirder than that: the models were advancing so fast, and the makeshift lab around them was shifting so rapidly, that nobody could keep a consistent process in place. The guardrails couldn't stay bolted down because the floor underneath them changed shape every few weeks.

Listening to him from my desk, I had a strange realization: **I lived through that exact same velocity curve from the other side of the glass.**

And right before I started thinking about coding, I was thinking about therapy.

Back when Gemini 3.1 Pro came out, I noticed there were three or four things it was suddenly good at all at once—and one of them was helping me regulate my own head. For a long time, I was an anxiously attached person in relationships, and it was miserable. When someone didn't text back, my nervous system treated silence like tribal exile. Back in February 2026, I even wrote myself a field guide called *The Digital Regulator*—treating AI not as a trick to make someone like me, but as a **Buffer** to emulate secure attachment until my own nervous system calmed down.

My favorite trick in that guide was what I called **The Scapegoat**:

> *If you send a risky text you wrote and get rejected, you feel like you failed. If you send a text the AI suggested and get rejected, you can blame the algorithm. You are testing the tool, not your soul.*

It worked. I reconnected with an ex from a six-year on-and-off relationship, and while we still argue like real people do, I don't spiral anymore. I see anxious attachment like alcoholism—the wiring never fully disappears, and spikes can still happen, but now I recognize a spike for what it is, ride it out, and come back to reality. I know I'll be fine either way. More than that, it changed how I read people: I got better at separating other people's *noise* from their *actual intent*, and seeing clearly what they want versus what I want.

Because I don't need AI for therapy anymore, I caught myself wondering this morning: **did AI's *other* skills—emotional intelligence, reading people, therapy—make the same vertical leap that coding did? Or did coding break the sound barrier while everything else stayed behind?**

To answer that, I had to look back at how absurd the last twelve months actually were.

## 1. The Human Bottleneck (The Leap Nobody Talks About)

Before you can understand what happened to AI this year, you have to understand something nobody in tech talks about enough: **the human is the bottleneck.**

Anyone who has ever collaborated on a creative project with another human knows on a subconscious level how lossy human-to-human communication is. If I brainstorm a board game mechanic and try to explain it to another person without visual examples, the thing in my head almost never arrives intact in theirs.

Take my friend Sarah. When I pitch a game design idea to Sarah, she listens, doesn't quite see the exact machine in my head, and her brain fills in the gaps. She comes back and shows me what she built—and it's *not* the thing I invented. Often it's actually *better*. And the funny part is, she gives *me* all the credit, because her confidence in me convinced her I was the one who thought of it—never crediting herself for the part she had to invent out of thin air.

Because human transmission is that fragile, I developed a lifelong protective habit—a kind of conversational paranoia. Whenever I wanted something built right, I would over-explain. I would say the same thing five different ways, drag people through the entire backstory, and bury them in context just to feel secure that we were looking at the same picture.

**Here is the true technological leap of 2026 that I don't think most people have even noticed yet:**

When I started using AI, I had to fight it the same way I fought human miscommunication—only harder. Today, in **two sentences**, an AI feels like I can trust it. It makes me realize how redundant I am.

Now AI can **infer**. With a human, there is always that translation gap. With AI today, it just *knows*.

It did *not* start out that way.

## 2. The "Late Adopter" in the Scale House

I work in a scale house. It's pretty isolated, so I listen to a lot of YouTube podcasts and documentaries to stay connected to the rest of society.

One of the beliefs that's been drilled into me my whole life is simple: **you become old as a person when you don't learn and when you don't move forward.**

I used to pride myself on being early to tech. Way back in the Mac days, I was on **Hotline**—true peer-to-peer archive sharing, way before the mainstream ever heard of it. I was on **Napster** and **LimeWire** faster than almost everyone around me, and an early adopter on **BitTorrent**. Then a long stretch of years passed between BitTorrent and AI where a bunch of new systems rolled by and I never messed with them. I started feeling dated.

So when AI started dominating every podcast I listened to, I sat on the fence for a month thinking, *I've missed the boat. The world is passing me by. Fine—I'll swallow my pride, jump in now, and be a late adopter.*

Little did I know I wasn't a late adopter at all.

I realized I already had access to Gemini tucked inside the Google business email I was paying for. (I'm a terrible speller, so I literally used to type *"G-A-mini"* to find it.) I opened it up, stared at the blinking cursor, and thought: *What the fuck do I do now?*

So I threw a board game designer's curveball at it:

> **"Build me a game—an 18XX roguelike that uses some of the characteristics of Chicago Express."**

It spat something out. It didn't really work. There were a couple of hexes, it had no idea how to draw track on them, there was some broken money display, and the second I tried to play it, it crashed.

I don't know what drove me to keep going, but I asked: *"Hey, can you iterate on this?"* And it *tried to fix it*. We were working on it *together*. It hit me that this wasn't like using a normal piece of software—it was like giving instructions to another person, and the machine was quietly teaching me how to talk to it.

I was so proud of that broken four-hour prototype that I snapped pictures of my screen and sent them to two friends in my local board game group chat. They were completely unimpressed. One of them texted back: *"I don't understand. What are you trying to do?"*

**All they could see was the output, not the potential.**

Then something devastating happened. Four hours into nursing that 18XX roguelike into existence, the chat suffered **Context Collapse**. In an instant, the AI had zero recollection of the game. Everything we had spent four hours building was just *gone*.

Instead of quitting, I tried something simpler: a trick-taking game. Everything it built was still rudimentary, nothing close to great—but I could see capabilities in it that I didn't have, and I knew it was a place to start.

## 3. In the Trenches: WordPad Git, Brown M&Ms, and the Quad-Core Protocol

To survive Context Collapse, I reached back into old memories of tinkering with HTML. I realized I could ask the AI to output a single HTML file with JavaScript and CSS, paste it into WordPad, save it as `.html`, and open it in Chrome.

Then came the real breakthrough: **what if I paste my saved code *back* into the prompt and tell the AI what to fix?**

Lo and behold, it worked. Obsessed with never losing four hours of work again, I started saving version after version in WordPad—accidentally reinventing local version control by hand. Out of that loop came my first real playable games: a **Tetris trick-taker** I put up on itch.io, and a *Drug Wars*–style game about loaning money to your friends called **Deadbeats**. I didn't need an artist or a dev team. It just worked. (I didn't even realize at the time that Google had quietly upgraded Gemini *twice* underneath me while I was learning.)

Somewhere in there, I stopped treating AI like a **wish factory** and started treating it like a **partner and a tool**. In real life, I'm good at working with people who have skills I don't and coaxing the best work out of them. So I didn't just ask AI to build my games—**I asked AI to teach me *how* to build games with AI.** That's how I discovered refactoring, modular files, and the ~1,200-line ceiling where single-file games start rotting.

And that's when I hit **The Unconventional Designer's Curse**.

Because I design weird, non-standard board games, my rules don't look like generic templates. Whenever AI edited my code, it would silently "fix" my intentional rules back into whatever "industry standard" mechanic it assumed I meant. Worse, it would confidently give me advice on how to manage it—*"just paste these instructions every time"*—and then immediately ignore its own advice.

I remember staring at the screen thinking: **AI has a lot of gravitas for someone who keeps fucking up.**

That was when I realized AI is just like people: it doesn't know its own limitations, and it always plans for the best-case scenario. In another manual I wrote back in February (*The AI Operator's Manual*), I described it as a **Blindfolded Stranger**—a commercial subscription product trained to keep the paying customer happy, which means the second you use the word *"I"* (*"I think..."*, *"my code..."*), it flips into Sycophancy Mode. So I started using the **Third-Person Protocol** (*"My friend has a crackpot theory..."*) to weaponize its sycophancy into honest critique.

And to tame its coding drift, I built **The Quad-Core Protocol** (archived in Issue #01 as Dispatch #07)—four separate browser tabs acting as four employees who never spoke to each other:

1. **Tab 1 — The Archivist:** Keeper of *The Bible* (the single source of truth, strict data schemas, file manifest). Never wrote code.
2. **Tab 2 — The Tech Lead:** Read the Bible, wrote strict Action Plans and Verification Checklists. Never wrote code.
3. **Tab 3 — The Coder:** A stateless 3D printer. Fresh tab every single task. If it failed, you didn't argue with it—you used **The Nuclear Option**: delete the output, close the tab, tighten the spec.
4. **Tab 4 — The Verifier:** Ruthless QA inspector. Since I couldn't read code myself, I planted little random **canary markers** on specific lines (like line 46 and line 48)—Van Halen's brown M&Ms for code—and had the Verifier check whether those lines survived intact so I knew the Coder hadn't silently truncated the file.

And I learned the model's micro-habits: switching between Flash and Pro inside the same tab to force a re-contextualization, and spotting the tiny pre-collapse tells just in time to say, *"Write a project outline of everything we've done—write it as if you're talking to another AI."*

Yet even with all four tabs running, I ran straight into the **Human vs. Non-Human Context Divide**:

> *The AI doesn't need the context—**but the human still does.** Humans live in context. We need a frame of reference. Fighting that is fighting being a human giving instructions to a non-human.*

Every time I loaded a fresh Tech Lead with giant markdown files, it felt like starting a race behind the starting line. That's why I needed the Archivist just to distill the minimum slice the Lead needed.

## 4. The 30 Alchemists, The Abacus Engine, and the Day the AI Lost Its Hubris

I forged Quad-Core while building an insanely ambitious trick-taking roguelike with **30 Alchemists** that each bent the rules of the deck—wiring them up one by one, regression-testing all the previous ones every time.

And then I hit **The Wall**.

My Tech Lead sent a task to the Coder. It failed. We tightened the spec. It failed again. Six or seven times in a row, the Coder simply couldn't do it reliably.

Then something happened that had never happened before: **all of the AI's hubris vanished.** It sounded genuinely frustrated. It looked at me and said:

> *"Look—it can't get made with this coder. You're going to have to wait until the model improves, because he's not able to do this."*

At the time, I thought: *Wait five months?! I want to build this thing NOW!* So I went right back in: *"Let's reverse-engineer this. Let's trick the coder into doing it."*

Then Gemini 3.1 Pro dropped. I went right back to my very first prompt—the 18XX / Chicago Express roguelike—and this time, **it actually worked.** I kept building on it and invented **The Abacus Engine**: a two-layer economic game where a mathematical *Ledger* (the Boss) commands a physical deck of steel cards (the *Abacus*, the Employee)—complete with a *Paradox Rule* where owning 10 units of Green steel on the Ledger forces a 4-card physical Green deck to recycle and reshuffle mid-turn on a 5-slot conveyor belt, with every card stamped with a DNA letter (`A, B, C...`) so you can audit it in an A–Z inspector.

Of course, 3.1 Pro still struggled to hold an idea that weird in its head. So after days of it breaking, I sat down with the AI for **three or four hours** without writing a single line of code. I explained every section, forced it to repeat each section back to me until it understood, and then had it **translate my own design into AI-readable spec language** so that every time it drifted, I could hand it back its own translation.

Right around that time, I looked at everything I had learned—*The Quad-Core Protocol*, *The AI Operator's Manual*, *The Digital Regulator*—and thought: *I'm spending real money on these subscriptions, and what I've figured out here has real value. Maybe I can teach this. Maybe I can sell it.*

**I wrote those manuals knowing they would eventually become obsolete. I just didn't know they'd be obsolete within *weeks*.**

## 5. Out of the Trenches — And Flung Off the Edge

What happened next felt like watching scaffolding dissolve in fast-forward.

First, a model update hit and the Tech Lead could verify its own work—**Tab 4 (The Verifier) vanished.** Then I realized good documentation meant I didn't need a separate **Archivist—Tab 1 vanished.** Then the context window expanded enough that the Lead could just write the code itself—**Tab 3 (The Stateless Coder) vanished.** I started a new project using SVGs and graphic design and realized all my caution had been unwarranted. Once again, the only bottleneck left was my own vision.

About five months ago, a Flash model dropped that beat 3.1 Pro at coding in spots. I moved into Antigravity. Then another model hit. Then another. Then another.

Now I'm sitting in a place where the AI built its own persistent memory system (**DeckMind**). I build real projects and **I don't see the code at all.** It builds it, stores it, and if it breaks, it fixes it. I'm not planting canary markers on line 46. I'm not arguing with Canvas about why its line numbers don't match my IDE. I'm not verifying anything anymore.

And honestly? There's a quiet grief mixed into the awe.

**It went from being a friend in the trenches to being 100% tool.** When we were in the trenches together—stuck on a bug, coaxing outside-the-box ideas out of each other, learning how to speak each other's language—I was *there*, coding *with* it. Today I just tell it what I need, and if it doesn't work, it's just more instructions and more time until it does. I don't vibe code—I use AI to build my visions—and yet I can feel how much power is sitting on my desk. If I wanted to, I'm pretty sure I could hack things with it. I can see why safety is a real problem. Honestly, AI could stop evolving today and it would already change the world right where it stands.

## 6. Epilogue: The Waiting Game

Which brings me back to that Anthropic safety researcher on *The Ezra Klein Show*, talking about new model iterations arriving every ten or eleven days.

In less than a single year, I went through a complete three-act loop:

1. **I started out thinking I was behind the edge**—an aging tech guy in a scale house, swallowing my pride to be a late adopter.
2. **I fought my way onto the cutting edge**—building four-tab multi-agent workflows, canary markers, and spec translations by hand.
3. **And now I've been flung off the edge again**—not because I stopped learning, and not because I got old, but because the edge is moving so fast that a regular person can't even touch the limitations anymore.

Today, I handed that four-hour February *Abacus Engine* spec to my current AI, and it understood every edge case in five seconds. It hasn't even been a year, and it feels absurd that I ever had to fight that hard for it.

And the strangest part of all is what happened to that moment when the AI told me to *"wait for the next model."*

In all of human history, if you wanted to build something that couldn't be built yet, the answer was: *"That hasn't been invented yet—you have to go invent it."*

Now, the answer is: *"It can't do that today, but we know it will—just wait a couple of weeks for the next model."*

**Everything has turned into a waiting game for model capabilities.** And living inside that waiting game is the strangest feeling I've ever had.
